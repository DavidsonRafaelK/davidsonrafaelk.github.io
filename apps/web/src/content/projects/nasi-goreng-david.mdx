I sell nasi goreng to my classmates. One menu, one price, ordered the day before
and paid the next day in class, by QRIS or cash.

It started small, the way these things do. A few friends asked if I could bring
some, then their friends asked, and pretty soon my WhatsApp was the order form.

## The problem was never the cooking

Nasi goreng sounds simple until you take orders for it. Everyone wants theirs a
little different. With or without vegetables. Spicy or not. And the egg is its
own conversation: dadar, ceplok matang, or ceplok setengah matang.

Nobody sends that as a neat list. They send it the way people actually text.
"2 ya, satu pedes satu engga, telurnya ceplok aja." Sometimes over three
messages. Sometimes the name comes an hour later. Sometimes they change their
mind after I've already written it down.

So every night I'd scroll back through every chat and rebuild the list by hand.
Who ordered, how many portions, which egg, spicy or not. It's not hard work, but
it's slow, and it's exactly the kind of work where you miss one message and
someone gets the wrong plate the next morning.

## Why not just use if-else

My first thought was to match keywords. If the message says "pedas", mark it
spicy. If it says "dadar", that's the egg.

I dropped that idea quickly. My classmates don't write in keywords. They write
"jangan pedes", "ga usah sayur", "ceplok yang setengah mateng ya". A keyword
matcher either misses those or forces people to type in a fixed format, and the
moment I tell my friends to order in a template, it stops feeling like texting a
friend and starts feeling like filling out a form. That was the whole reason
they ordered from me in the first place.

What I needed was something that could read a normal chat and understand it.

## Bringing in Gemini

So I built an assistant with the Gemini API and connected it to WhatsApp. I
named her Serena.

Serena reads what a customer sends, works out what they want, and asks for
whatever is still missing in one message instead of five. Once the order is
complete she shows a summary with the total, and only saves it after the
customer says yes. She's honest about what she is, too. If someone asks whether
they're talking to a person, she says she's Nasi Goreng David's AI assistant.

The part that matters most to me is the recap. Every order lands in a structured
list instead of scattered chats, and the bot summarizes it for me. Now every
morning I just open the recap and cook. No scrolling, no rebuilding the list
from memory, no "wait, did you say ceplok or dadar?"

For my classmates, nothing about ordering got harder. They still text me like
they always did, in their own words, whenever they want. They just get a reply
right away instead of waiting until I see the message between classes.

## Making it last

A side business has a budget of roughly zero, so I rebuilt the bot to be cheap
to run and harder to break.

The new version is written in Rust. Plain code runs the ordering steps: asking
for missing details, showing the summary, saving, cancelling. A small model
running locally handles reading routine orders, so a normal order doesn't cost
anything.

Gemini handles the part that actually needs a conversation: the questions that
aren't orders. "Kak, besok bisa bayar pakai QRIS?" "Is it still available
tomorrow?" Gemini also steps in as the backup reader whenever the local model
can't be reached, so a customer never gets ignored because my laptop is having a
bad day.

There are a few small rules that came straight from running the business. At
9 PM the bot sends me the day's recap. At 5 PM, yesterday's orders that are
still waiting for payment are marked complete, because by then everyone has paid
me in class. And each customer is capped at 20 messages an hour, because
classmates are classmates.

The rebuilt version hasn't taken live orders yet. The idea it's built on
already has.

## What it taught me

The thing I keep coming back to is that the AI didn't replace anything my
customers liked. It replaced the part I didn't like: the late-night scroll
through chat history. My classmates still get to text me like a friend. I just
don't have to be the one who turns those texts into a list anymore.

That's the kind of AI use I want to show people on campus as a Google Student
Ambassador. Not something flashy. A real, small problem that a student actually
has, solved with a tool anyone can start using today.
